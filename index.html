About me

I am a Postdoctoral Research Associate in Amy Trowbridge's lab in the Department of Forest and Wildlife Ecology at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where my research focuses on forest pathogen and pest defense.

I completed my PhD at the University of British Columbia, where my work focused broadly on carbon fluxes at three scales: the individual tree level, across elevational gradients, and the ecosystem level. Each of my three thesis chapters took one of these focuses. I am interested in how plants adapt to the changing climate and studied this via carbon fluxes and gas exchange.

Research

Branch excision & gas exchange

Branch excision is widely used for measuring gas exchange in tall canopies, but its effects on the measurements themselves have been unclear. We collected paired measurements of gas exchange and water potential on intact and excised branches across species spanning a range of drought strategies and xylem transport mechanisms. Excision relieved tension in the water column and generally decreased photosynthesis and stomatal conductance, so we recommend that studies estimate excision effects for their own species and sites before adopting the method.

Published in Tree Physiology: read the paper.

Ecosystem fluxes in extreme heat

Climate models assume photosynthesis and transpiration are tightly coupled, with stomata closing to conserve water when photosynthesis is limited by extreme heat. Using AmeriFlux eddy covariance data from the record-breaking 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, we found that above 20–25 °C gross primary productivity declined to near zero while evapotranspiration continued to increase — evidence that transpiration acts as a leaf-cooling mechanism during heatwaves, with important implications for land surface models.

Carbon–water coupling across climates

How do plants coordinate carbon gain and water loss as temperatures rise? Using dense assimilation–temperature response curves measured across elevational gradients spanning hot-dry to cool-wet climates in South Africa and Norway, we found that plants decouple carbon gain from water loss above their thermal optimum — most strongly in hot, dry conditions. Thermal response parameters were invariant across climates, suggesting that plant responses to heat involve biophysical regulation and water-mediated cooling rather than acclimation of photosynthetic machinery.

Electrical signalling & tree health

Plants produce electrical signals, but their relationship to plant health has been unclear. By measuring amperage rather than voltage, we characterized the electrical stress signatures produced by Douglas-fir trees in response to mechanical wounding and showed that healthy and unhealthy trees produce distinct signatures — a promising method for monitoring and early detection of changes in forest health.
